*Written by David Robertson, Online Strength Coach*

A disappointing workout does not mean your strength program has stopped working.
Neither does one week without adding weight.

Strength does not rise in a straight line. Your performance can change with the lift,
technique, equipment, training phase, sleep, meals, schedule, and stress outside the
gym. That makes a plateau a pattern to investigate—not a verdict from one session.

The useful question is not simply, “How do I break this plateau?” It is:

> What does my training trend show, what was happening around that training, and which
> constraint is most likely to matter now?

Answering that well requires more than a new routine or a wearable score. It requires
comparable training data and an honest conversation about your circumstances.

## First, make sure you are measuring the same thing

Before changing your program, check whether the apparent stall is based on comparable
performances.

Ask:

- Was it the same exercise variation?
- Did you use the same range of motion and technique standard?
- Was the equipment comparable?
- Are you comparing working sets, an estimated one-repetition maximum, or a true test?
- Did the number of sets, repetitions, rest periods, or exercise order change?
- Is this a trend across several exposures, or one unusually poor session?

For example, a technically stricter squat at the same load is not necessarily “no
progress.” A heavier top set followed by fewer repetitions in later sets is also not the
same test as repeating last week’s workout.

There is no universal number of workouts or weeks that proves you have plateaued. The
right comparison window depends on your training experience, the lift, the program, and
how often comparable performances occur.

## Check what was planned against what actually happened

A program cannot be assessed only from the exercises written on the page. Start with the
training you completed.

Review:

- planned and completed sessions;
- working loads, sets, and repetitions;
- repeated substitutions;
- missed sessions and longer interruptions;
- whether the program’s progression rules were followed; and
- how often you restarted or replaced the routine.

This is not a test of willpower. A demanding job, travel, family responsibilities, or
limited equipment can make a theoretically good program a poor fit in practice. The
solution may be to make the plan more realistic—not to blame yourself or add more work.

## Training data needs a conversation

A training log can show that performance changed. It usually cannot explain why on its
own.

That is why my review process combines the external demand with the client’s internal
response:

- **External demand** is the work prescribed and completed: exercises, loads, sets,
  repetitions, frequency, and other measurable work.
- **Internal response** is the person’s physiological and psychological response to
  that work, including how demanding it felt in their current circumstances.

This distinction follows a published training-load framework, but the framework does
not turn either side into a complete diagnosis.[^8]

The discussion around the data can include sleep, meals, schedule changes, work and
family pressure, travel, illness, motivation, and perceived effort. The relevant
stressors differ from one client to another.

Wearable data can add context, but it does not settle the question. A heart-rate
variability value or proprietary recovery score should not override the training trend
and the client’s experience. Research in endurance athletes has found that some
heart-rate-variability changes can occur with both improved performance and
training-related overreaching, so additional measures are needed to interpret the
signal.[^9]

In other words, the same chart can lead to different decisions for two people. Context
is not noise around the “real” data. It is part of the evidence.

## Review the training stimulus

If the performances are comparable and the program was followed, examine whether the
training still provides an appropriate stimulus for the outcome.

### Is there a clear progression method?

Continued adaptation requires progressive training, but progression does not mean
adding weight at every workout forever. Depending on the program, progression can
involve load, repetitions, sets, execution, exercise selection, or how work is arranged
over time.

The American College of Sports Medicine’s position stand recommends progressive
resistance training and describes broader, periodized loading for intermediate and
advanced trainees.[^1] That supports a general principle, not one perfect progression
formula for every person.

### Does the loading match the goal?

Strength is specific. If the goal is to improve maximal strength in a lift, the program
usually needs meaningful exposure to that movement and to appropriately heavy work.

A 12-week study comparing several intensities found improvements across conditions, but
the heavier conditions produced larger strength gains in that sample.[^4] The study
involved 30 men and does not establish one universal percentage. It does illustrate why
training only with very light loads may not be the best match for a maximal-strength
goal.

### Is there enough useful volume—and can you recover from it?

Weekly volume can affect strength development. A meta-analysis found that medium and
higher weekly set volumes produced greater strength gains than low volume in its
included studies.[^2] However, only nine studies met its criteria, and the result does
not mean that continually adding sets will solve every plateau.

Volume has to be relevant, repeatable, and compatible with the client’s response and
circumstances. If the current dose is too low, more useful work may help. If session
quality is falling while life demands are unusually high, adding work may make the
problem harder to interpret.

### Would a different frequency organize the work better?

Training frequency is often discussed as if there is one ideal number of sessions per
week. The evidence is less simple.

A systematic review found that higher frequencies were associated with greater strength
gains, but the effect was largely explained by the extra training volume. When volume
was equated, frequency alone did not have a significant effect.[^3]

Frequency can still be useful. It can distribute weekly work across shorter sessions,
create more opportunities to practice a lift, or fit training around a client’s
schedule. That is an organizational decision, not a magic number.

## You do not have to take every set to failure

When progress stalls, a common reaction is to make every set harder. Training to failure
is one option, but it is not a requirement for strength progress.

A systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant overall difference between
failure and non-failure training for strength or hypertrophy. The authors concluded that
failure was not required for either outcome, while also noting that the available
studies involved young adults.[^5]

The practical question is not, “Did I suffer enough?” It is whether the work was
sufficiently challenging, technically useful, and recoverable inside the larger plan.

## Planned variation is different from changing routines randomly

Repeating exactly the same stress indefinitely is not the only alternative to program
hopping.

I use planned changes in training demands across time. The technical term is
**undulating periodization**: volume and intensity vary within a coherent plan rather
than increasing in a straight line or changing at random.

A meta-analysis of volume-equated programs found a greater effect on one-repetition
maximum strength from periodized than non-periodized resistance training. Its subgroup
findings suggested that trained participants may benefit from daily or weekly variation,
but the analysis did not show a hypertrophy advantage and does not establish one best
model for everyone.[^6]

The purpose of variation is not “muscle confusion.” Each phase should still connect to
the goal, the previous work, and what the trainee can currently tolerate.

Daily adjustments can also sit inside a longer plan. A systematic review found that
subjective and objective autoregulation methods may support maximal-strength
development.[^7] This can help account for day-to-day changes in performance, but it is
not a reason to rewrite the program whenever a workout feels difficult.

## A consultation-led way to troubleshoot a plateau

Use the following sequence as an assessment, not an automatic prescription:

1. **Choose one outcome.** Identify the lift or performance measure you are evaluating.
2. **Build a comparable trend.** Review the same movement, standard, and type of
   performance across an appropriate window.
3. **Check execution.** Compare planned training with completed training.
4. **Discuss the context.** Identify relevant changes in sleep, meals, work, family,
   travel, health, motivation, schedule, and perceived effort.
5. **Compare demand and response.** Ask whether the completed work appears too little,
   poorly matched, or difficult to recover from in the client’s circumstances.
6. **Form a working hypothesis.** Choose the most plausible constraint instead of
   changing everything.
7. **Agree on one realistic adjustment.** Define what evidence would support or weaken
   the hypothesis.
8. **Monitor and discuss the response.** Keep other variables reasonably stable and
   review the next comparable trend with the client.

This process may point toward more volume, less volume, a different loading range,
better exercise specificity, redistributed sessions, or no immediate program change.
The decision depends on the person and the evidence—not the label “plateau.”

## Fictional example: the log did not tell the whole story

> and approved by David Robertson before publication.

Jordan has repeated the same squat working load for three comparable sessions. The
first reaction is to add sets and switch to a new squat program.

The log shows a different starting point:

| Review item | Fictional observation |
|---|---|
| Exercise standard | Same squat variation, equipment, and range of motion |
| Attendance | All planned sessions completed |
| Working performance | Same load; completed repetitions fell in the final set |
| Perceived effort | Higher than in the previous training phase |
| Recent context | Major work deadline, shorter sleep, and several missed meals |
| Wearable trend | Lower recovery scores, treated as context rather than a diagnosis |

The external work has not increased, while the reported cost of completing it has. In a
check-in, Jordan explains that the work deadline will continue for another two weeks.

That conversation changes the working hypothesis. The program may not need a dramatic
overhaul, and adding volume immediately would make the result harder to interpret.
Jordan and the coach agree on one temporary, realistic adjustment, define what they will
monitor, and schedule another review after comparable sessions under more stable
conditions.

The example does not prove that work stress or sleep caused the stalled squat. It shows
why the next decision cannot be read from the load alone.

## When outside help may be useful

You may benefit from coaching when:

- you have enough training history but cannot interpret it in context;
- your schedule, equipment, or stressors keep making static templates fail;
- you change several variables at once and cannot tell what worked;
- you need a program that responds to your constraints without becoming random; or
- you want an external view of your progression and program fit.

My [online strength coaching](/) connects logged training data with regular client
consultation. The aim is not to promise a universal plateau breaker. It is to make the
next training decision more deliberate and better matched to you.

## Scope and safety

Strength coaching does not diagnose or treat pain, injuries, or medical conditions. If
pain, injury, illness, or a medical condition affects your training, seek advice and
clearance from an appropriately qualified healthcare professional. Coaching should then
remain within that guidance and the scope of strength coaching.
