How to Plan Your Strength Training Week. Written by David Robertson, online strength coach.
Already following a strength program, but struggling to fit its sessions into your week.
Start by comparing the training you planned with the training you completed.
How do I fit my strength program into the week?
The biggest planning mistake I see in strength training is committing to a training program
without deciding when its sessions will happen. Saying you will train four times a week gives you
a target. Choosing the days gives you a schedule you can check against what actually happens.
Take Alex, a fictional executive who wants to squat more weight for five reps.
His program calls for four strength sessions a week, including two lower body sessions.
Alex schedules them for Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday, and commits to that schedule for the next
three weeks. At four sessions a week, he has 12 planned workouts. That gives him a clear starting
point for checking how consistently he follows the program. The problem.
It's tempting to play catch-up when you miss a training session. Sometimes you can move it
without disrupting the rest of the week. Repeated changes, though, can crowd sessions together
or push them into days already claimed by other commitments. That can make recovery and the
remaining sessions harder to manage. Keep your original plan alongside what you actually did,
so you can see whether a day needs to change rather than continually trying to catch up.
How do I build my weekly training matrix? A training matrix is a simple table showing which work out
belongs on which day. Start with the frequency specified in your program, then look at the days
and time you have available. Include the time each session needs. For a four day split like
Alex's, I usually recommend Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday. Here is how those sessions fit
together. Each of the three weeks follows the same schedule. Monday is the first upper body session.
Tuesday is the first lower body session. Wednesday is a day off. Thursday is the second upper body
session and Friday is the second lower body session. Saturday and Sunday are days off. This is an
illustrative structure. Session names, order, frequency, and spacing depend on the trainee in the
program. Before the three weeks begin, check for meetings, deadlines, and travel you already know
about. Then look back at the last few weeks, which days were hardest to keep free for training.
If Mondays and Thursdays are likely to be busy, Alex could choose Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday,
and Saturday before the block begins, keeping the workouts in the same order.
The plan still includes four strength sessions each week, now on days that better fit his schedule.
Your training week needs room for more than workouts. Work, preparing food, time with other
people, time alone, and winding down before sleep all affect what fits. Consider the fatigue from
training alongside the demands of the rest of your life. When the same session repeatedly gets
missed, review whether it's timing and demands fit your week. Aim for a sustainable rhythm that
allows for recovery and unexpected changes. What should I record after each session?
Keep your plan matrix and record what happened alongside it. For each session, note whether you
completed it, the day you trained, and any reason it was missed, moved, or shortened. Keep
recording your exercises, weights, sets, and reps in your workout log. If you move Tuesdays workout
to Wednesday, record both the plan day and the actual day. You still completed the workout,
but the original Tuesday slot did not fit that week as well as you expected.
That distinction helps you choose a better schedule at your review. Likewise,
mark a shortened session as shortened and note what you left out. A weekly count is useful,
but it will not tell you whether you repeatedly miss the part of the program that matters most to
your goal. What if I miss one of my plan days? On Tuesday, Alex's leadership meeting moves into
the time he set aside for his lower body session. By the time he gets home, he has a short window
before dinner. He does a 20-minute conditioning workout at home and keeps his next lower body
session on Friday. He records Tuesday's strength session as missed and logs the conditioning separately.
He stayed active, but he did not complete the strength workout in his matrix.
Keeping that record accurate is what makes the review useful. For this example, I'd keep the
rest of his week in place. Trying to fit the missed leg workout into the weekend would put it
closer to Friday's session than planned. Whether to move a workout depends on the program and
the time available to recover. One miss session is also worth distinguishing from a recurring problem.
If Tuesday keeps getting interrupted, write that down. At the next review,
you can decide whether Tuesday is a realistic training day and adjust the schedule accordingly.
What can three weeks tell me about my schedule? Check your record at the end of each week,
then review the three weeks together. For Alex, an illustrative record might look like this.
In week one, Alex planned four strength sessions and completed all four on the planned days.
In week two, he planned four and completed three, missing Tuesday because of a meeting.
In week three, he planned four and completed three, missing Tuesday because of a work deadline.
Across the three weeks, he planned 12 strength sessions and completed 10.
Alex completed 10 of 12 planned strength sessions or about 83%.
More useful than the percentage alone is the pattern. Both missed sessions were Tuesday's lower
body workout. His overall attendance hides a repeated gap in the work tied to his squat goal.
Two other versions of the same three week plan show why it helps to record both completed sessions
and the days on which they happened. Alex originally planned to train on Monday,
Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday in each version. These are illustrative outcomes,
not predictions about what moving a workout will cause. In the first version,
Alex moves Tuesday's lower body session to Wednesday in weeks two and three.
In week one, he completes the four sessions on the original days.
In each of weeks two and three, he trains upper body on Monday, lower body on Wednesday,
upper body on Thursday, and lower body on Friday.
He completes four strength sessions in each week and all 12 across the three weeks.
That is 100% session adherence. 10 of the 12 workouts happen on the days he originally chose,
so his planned day adherence is about 83%. But the completed count does not show how close the
sessions are. Moving the first lower body workout from Tuesday to Wednesday leaves about 48 hours
before Friday's second lower body workout, rather than about 72 hours. It also puts workouts
on Wednesday and Thursday back to back. For Alex's strength goal, that shorter gap could leave
him less ready for Friday's lower body work, especially of both sessions involve hard squats.
If he were training mainly to build muscle and each session included many hard sets,
the same change could make fatigue harder to manage. Neither outcome is automatic. The exercises,
number of sets, effort, and Alex's recovery all matter. His workout log can show whether he is
still completing the later sessions as intended. Wednesday may be a better day to choose for the next
block, but only if the recovery between sessions also fits. In the second version, Alex misses sessions
and cannot fit them in later. He completes all four planned workouts in week one. In weeks two and
three, he completes Mondays and Thursday's upper body sessions, but misses Tuesdays and Friday's
lower body sessions. That is two completed workouts out of four in each of those weeks,
and eight out of 12 overall. That is about 67% session adherence. In this hypothetical version,
he hopes to catch up on the missed sessions, but cannot fit them around his other commitments
and recovery. The percentage describes what he completed. It does not prove that trying to move
sessions caused the missed workouts. His record shows which parts of the plan need another look
before the next three weeks. Missing those sessions reduces the training Alex actually completes.
It does not itself increase fatigue. A missed session in week one would not, on its own,
create extra fatigue by week three either. The recovery concern arises if he tries to make up that
work by crowding hard sessions into later weeks, especially if the plan sets or loads are also rising.
Review attendance alongside weights, reps, and recovery before deciding whether to move a session,
change the plan, or leave a missed workout missed. Ask yourself, how many of my plan sessions
did I complete each week? Did I train on the days I chose, or did I repeatedly move sessions?
Did the same workout or part of a workout keep getting missed? What got in the way,
and what could I change for the next three weeks? Three weeks gives you a practical starting point
for reviewing how your schedule fits your life. It will not capture every kind of disruption,
so keep reviewing as your circumstances change. Use the record alongside your workout
log to understand both what you completed and how your training is progressing.
What should I change for the next three weeks? If you completed the sessions on your chosen days
and the schedule still fits, you have a reason to keep those days. You do not need to rearrange
the week simply because a review date has arrived. If a particular day repeatedly proved difficult,
look for a more reliable slot. Alex might move to Monday, Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday if
those days fit his upcoming commitments better. Write the revised schedule down before the next
three weeks begin so you can review how the revised schedule works. If you plan four sessions each
week but consistently completed two, look at why. Was it the days, the length of the sessions,
or the total time the program required? Review the program against the time and recovery you have
available. Simply leaving out half of a four-day split may repeatedly leave out the same training.
Keep the original plan in your record. Changing next week's schedule does not change what you
planned or completed last week. How does your weekly record guide future training? Once you know
which sessions you can reliably attend, you have a clearer starting point for planning how the
training itself changes over time. Periodization means planning changes in training over time,
including the amount of work and how heavy it is to support a goal. Your weekly matrix sets out when
the sessions happen. The program sets out the work within them. At a review, use attendance,
work out results, and recovery together to guide the next phase. A repeated scheduling problem and
a need to change the training load call for different decisions. Keeping a record helps you see which
question you need to address. What should I put in my matrix today? Write down your training goal
and the weekly frequency your program specifies. Choose the days and times you will train. Put each
session in the matrix and set a review date three weeks from now. Then keep a record. At your review,
you will have a clearer answer to a practical question. How well did my plan training fit my week?
