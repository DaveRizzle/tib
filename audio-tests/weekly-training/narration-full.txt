How to Plan Your Strength Training Week.

Written by David Robertson, Online Strength Coach.

Already following a strength program, but struggling to fit its sessions into your week? Start by comparing the training you planned with the training you completed.

How do I fit my strength program into the week?

The biggest planning mistake I see in strength training is committing to a training program without deciding when its sessions will happen.

Saying you will train four times a week gives you a number. Putting those four sessions on specific days gives you a schedule you can review. At the end of the week, you can compare what you intended to do with what you actually completed and see how well the plan fits your circumstances.

Take Alex, a fictional executive who wants to squat more weight for five reps. His program calls for four strength sessions a week, including two lower-body sessions. He puts them on Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday, and commits to that schedule for the next three weeks.

At four sessions a week, he has twelve planned workouts. That gives him a clear starting point for checking how consistently he follows the program.

How do I build my weekly training matrix?

A training matrix is a simple table showing which workout belongs on which day. Start with the frequency specified in your program, then look at the days and time you have available. Include the time each session needs.

For a four-day split like Alex's, I usually recommend Monday, Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday. Here is how those sessions fit together.

Each of the three weeks follows the same schedule. Monday is the first upper-body session. Tuesday is the first lower-body session. Wednesday is a day off. Thursday is the second upper-body session, and Friday is the second lower-body session. Saturday and Sunday are days off.

This is an illustrative structure. Session names, order, frequency, and spacing depend on the trainee and the program.

Before the three weeks begin, check for meetings, deadlines, and travel you already know about. Then look back at the last few weeks. Which days were hardest to keep free for training?

If Mondays and Thursdays are likely to be busy, Alex could choose Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday before the block begins, keeping the workouts in the same order. The plan still includes four strength sessions each week, now on days that better fit his schedule.

Your training week needs room for more than workouts. Work, preparing food, time with other people, time alone, and winding down before sleep all affect what fits. Consider the fatigue from training alongside the demands of the rest of your life.

When the same session repeatedly gets missed, review whether its timing and demands fit your week. Aim for a sustainable rhythm that allows for recovery and unexpected changes.

What should I record after each session?

Keep your planned matrix and record what happened alongside it. For each session, note whether you completed it, the day you trained, and any reason it was missed, moved, or shortened. Keep recording your exercises, weights, sets, and reps in your workout log.

If you move Tuesday's workout to Wednesday, record both the planned day and the actual day. You still completed the workout, but the original Tuesday slot did not fit that week as well as you expected. That distinction helps you choose a better schedule at your review.

Likewise, mark a shortened session as shortened and note what you left out. A weekly count is useful, but it will not tell you whether you repeatedly missed the part of the program that matters most to your goal.

What if I miss one of my planned days?

On Tuesday, Alex's leadership meeting moves into the time he set aside for his lower-body session. By the time he gets home, he has a short window before dinner. He does a twenty-minute conditioning workout at home and keeps his next lower-body session on Friday.

He records Tuesday's strength session as missed and logs the conditioning separately. He stayed active, but he did not complete the strength workout in his matrix. Keeping that record accurate is what makes the review useful.

For this example, I'd keep the rest of his week in place. Trying to fit the missed leg workout into the weekend would put it closer to Friday's session than planned. Whether to move a workout depends on the program and the time available to recover.

One missed session is also worth distinguishing from a recurring problem. If Tuesday keeps getting interrupted, write that down. At the next review, you can decide whether Tuesday is a realistic training day and adjust the schedule accordingly.

What can three weeks tell me about my schedule?

Check your record at the end of each week, then review the three weeks together. For Alex, an illustrative record might look like this:

In week one, Alex planned four strength sessions and completed all four on the planned days. In week two, he planned four and completed three, missing Tuesday because of a meeting. In week three, he planned four and completed three, missing Tuesday because of a work deadline. Across the three weeks, he planned twelve strength sessions and completed ten.

Alex completed ten of twelve planned strength sessions, or about eighty-three percent. More useful than the percentage alone is the pattern: both missed sessions were Tuesday's lower-body workout. His overall attendance hides a repeated gap in the work tied to his squat goal.

Ask yourself:

How many of my planned sessions did I complete each week?

Did I train on the days I chose, or did I repeatedly move sessions?

Did the same workout or part of a workout keep getting missed?

What got in the way, and what could I change for the next three weeks?

Three weeks gives you a practical starting point for reviewing how your schedule fits your life. It will not capture every kind of disruption, so keep reviewing as your circumstances change. Use the record alongside your workout log to understand both what you completed and how your training is progressing.

What should I change for the next three weeks?

If you completed the sessions on your chosen days and the schedule still fits, you have a reason to keep those days. You do not need to rearrange the week simply because a review date has arrived.

If a particular day repeatedly proved difficult, look for a more reliable slot. Alex might move to Monday, Wednesday, Friday, and Saturday if those days fit his upcoming commitments better. Write the revised schedule down before the next three weeks begin, so you can review how the revised schedule works.

If you planned four sessions each week but consistently completed two, look at why. Was it the days, the length of the sessions, or the total time the program required? Review the program against the time and recovery you have available. Simply leaving out half of a four-day split may repeatedly leave out the same training.

Keep the original plan in your record. Changing next week's schedule does not change what you planned or completed last week.

How does this lead into periodization?

Once you know which sessions you can reliably attend, you have a clearer starting point for planning how the training itself changes over time.

Periodization means planning changes in training over time, including the amount of work and how heavy it is, to support a goal. Your weekly matrix sets out when the sessions happen; the program sets out the work within them.

At a review, use attendance, workout results, and recovery together to guide the next phase. A repeated scheduling problem and a need to change the training load call for different decisions. Keeping a record helps you see which question you need to address.

What should I put in my matrix today?

Write down your training goal and the weekly frequency your program specifies. Choose the days and times you will train, put each session in the matrix, and set a review date three weeks from now.

Then keep a record. At your review, you will have a clearer answer to a practical question: how well did my planned training fit my week?
